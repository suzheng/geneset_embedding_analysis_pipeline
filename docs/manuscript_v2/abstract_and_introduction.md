Functional Mapping of Sepsis Immune Dysregulation and Therapeutic Targets Using Large-Scale Gene Representation Learning
Abstract
Background Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of dysregulated host response to infection that kills millions annually, characterized by the simultaneous occurrence of hyperinflammation and immune suppression—a duality that has frustrated efforts to develop effective immunomodulatory therapies. Genomic and transcriptomic studies have become central tools for uncovering disease mechanisms and identifying candidate therapeutics, and gene set enrichment analysis is among the most widely used methods for interpreting such data. However, conventional approaches rely on direct gene symbol overlap and therefore miss functional relationships between gene sets that share biological roles but not the same genes.
Methods We applied a gene representation-based computational framework in which sepsis-associated genes were translated into numerical representations learned from gene expression of 410K samples, enabling systematic discovery of robust functional relationships beyond direct gene overla. They position genes with similar biological functions close together in a shared mathematical space, and compared the resulting functional signature against five curated reference libraries covering biological pathways, immune cell types, drug signatures, and disease profiles.
Results The sepsis signature most closely matched T helper 17 (Th17) cells alongside functionally exhausted CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, together capturing the coexisting hyperinflammation and immunoparalysis that characterize clinical sepsis. Innate immune populations including monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils were also prominently represented. Disease signature analysis revealed a molecular convergence between sepsis and Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, suggesting shared mechanisms of epithelial injury. Drug signature analysis nominated ruxolitinib, a JAK inhibitor, as a candidate therapeutic agent.
Conclusion Gene representation-based analysis of sepsis-associated genes reveals concurrent Th17-driven inflammation and T cell exhaustion, a molecular convergence with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, and nominates JAK inhibition as a rationale-supported therapeutic strategy.

Introduction
Sepsis remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern critical care medicine, accounting for more than 11 million deaths annually worldwide and representing a leading cause of in-hospital mortality despite decades of intensive research [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(19)32989-7]. Defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.0287], sepsis is distinguished from uncomplicated infection not merely by the severity of the pathogen burden but by the maladaptive nature of the host immune response itself.
Early conceptual frameworks cast sepsis primarily as a hyperinflammatory disorder, in which an uncontrolled cytokine storm—driven by innate immune pattern recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns—was thought to be the principal cause of organ injury and death. This model motivated numerous clinical trials targeting individual pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor, interleukin-1, and high-mobility group box 1 protein. With very few exceptions, these trials failed to improve mortality, suggesting that hyperinflammation alone did not capture the full complexity of sepsis pathophysiology [https://doi.org/10.1086/339549].
Contemporary understanding has substantially revised this view. It is now recognized that sepsis is characterized not by unidirectional hyperactivation but by a simultaneous and paradoxical combination of excessive inflammation and profound immune suppression, often termed immunoparalysis [https://doi.org/10.1038/nri3552]. These opposing immune states may coexist within the same patient and even within the same tissue compartment, reflecting the extraordinary heterogeneity of the septic response. Pro-inflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells drive neutrophil recruitment and amplify cytokine production at sites of infection, contributing to collateral tissue damage in severe sepsis [https://doi.org/10.1002/jcla.23753]. At the same time, persistent antigen stimulation in the septic environment can exhaust adaptive immune cells, particularly CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which progressively lose effector function and upregulate inhibitory checkpoint receptors [https://doi.org/10.1038/nri3552]. T cell exhaustion is now recognized as a hallmark of sepsis-induced immunoparalysis and has been independently associated with secondary infections, prolonged intensive care unit stay, and increased mortality [https://doi.org/10.1038/nri3552].
This immunological duality—active inflammation occurring concurrently with immune dysfunction—has profound implications for therapy. Strategies that broadly suppress inflammation risk deepening immunoparalysis, whereas approaches aimed at restoring immune function may exacerbate organ injury in patients who remain hyperinflamed. The failure to account for this heterogeneity is widely considered a central reason for the poor translation of promising preclinical immunomodulatory strategies into effective clinical therapies. Identifying the molecular underpinnings of each immune state, and understanding how they interact, is therefore a prerequisite for developing precision therapeutic strategies in sepsis [https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2018.01926].
Transcriptomic approaches—particularly whole-blood and tissue-level RNA sequencing—have substantially advanced our ability to characterize immune states in septic patients [https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-2600(16)30004-1]. However, the interpretation of sepsis gene expression data is complicated by substantial inter-patient variability, the influence of comorbidities, and the evolving nature of the immune response over the clinical course of sepsis. Standard gene set enrichment methods, which assess whether a gene set is statistically overrepresented in a ranked list of differentially expressed genes or whether two gene sets share more members than expected by chance, depend critically on direct gene symbol overlap [https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0506580102]. These approaches may fail to capture functional similarity between gene sets that share biological roles—such as participation in the same signaling pathway or regulation of the same cellular process—but differ in their specific gene composition across studies or experimental contexts. Contemporary large-scale computational models now allow gene functions to be inferred from population-scale expression data, providing a more comprehensive and less bias-prone view of disease biology than traditional single-cohort analyses.
To overcome this limitation, we employed an embedding-based computational framework leveraging deep learning models trained on 410,000 human RNA-seq samples, enabling unbiased characterization of gene functional relationships across diverse biological contexts. In the model, each gene is represented as a point in a high-dimensional numerical space learned from large-scale human transcriptomic data. In this representation, genes with similar biological functions, co-expression patterns, and regulatory relationships are positioned close to one another in the embedding space, regardless of whether they share identical gene symbols. By summarizing a gene set as a statistical signature in this space—capturing both the collective functional profile and the internal coherence of the set—it becomes possible to compare a query gene set against thousands of reference gene sets representing pathways, cell types, diseases, and drug responses, even in the absence of direct gene overlap.
In this study, we applied this framework to a curated set of sepsis-associated genes to construct a functional sepsis signature and map it systematically against five curated biological reference libraries.
