Immune Cell Types Involved in Sepsis

To identify which immune cell types are involved in the sepsis response, the sepsis-associated gene signature was compared against the CellMarker 2024 library, which contains 1,692 curated cell type marker signatures representing distinct immune and non-immune cell populations across human and mouse tissues. Cell type matching was performed using variance-weighted distance calculations across selected embedding dimensions, where smaller distance values indicate greater similarity between the sepsis signature and cell type marker signatures. The analysis revealed strong associations with multiple immune cell populations that play central roles in the inflammatory response during sepsis.

The highest-ranked cell type match was T Helper 17 (Th17) Cell (distance: 3.428, cosine similarity: 0.820), a pro-inflammatory T cell subset that produces interleukin-17 and plays a critical role in host defense against extracellular pathogens. Th17 cells are known to be involved in severe inflammatory responses and have been implicated in sepsis pathophysiology. Additional T cell populations were also highly ranked, including Exhausted CD8+ T Cell (distance: 4.253, cosine similarity: 0.663) and Exhausted CD4+ T Cell (distance: 4.354, cosine similarity: 0.647), which represent T cell states characterized by reduced effector function that can develop during chronic or severe infections. These exhausted T cell signatures suggest that sepsis may be associated with T cell dysfunction, which is clinically relevant as T cell exhaustion has been linked to poor outcomes in sepsis patients. A general T Cell signature was also identified (distance: 4.262, cosine similarity: 0.718), indicating broad T cell involvement in the sepsis response.

Myeloid cell populations were prominently represented among the top matches. Monocyte signatures were highly ranked, with Monocyte Bone Marrow Mouse showing the strongest match (distance: 3.885, cosine similarity: 0.697) among myeloid cells. Macrophage Lung Mouse was also identified (distance: 4.104, cosine similarity: 0.724), reflecting the importance of tissue-resident macrophages in the immune response. Neutrophil populations were represented by Stage II Neutrophil Bone Marrow Mouse (distance: 4.197, cosine similarity: 0.662) and Neutrophil Blood Human (distance: 4.299, cosine similarity: 0.720), indicating neutrophil involvement in sepsis, which aligns with the clinical observation that neutrophils are among the first responders to infection and play a central role in sepsis pathophysiology. Mast Cell Bone Marrow Mouse (distance: 3.562, cosine similarity: 0.746) and Basophil Undefined Human (distance: 4.160, cosine similarity: 0.725) were also highly ranked, representing granulocyte populations involved in allergic and inflammatory responses. The broad Myeloid Cell Bone Marrow Human signature (distance: 4.252, cosine similarity: 0.695) and Myeloid Cell Bone Marrow Mouse signature (distance: 4.377, cosine similarity: 0.696) were identified, reflecting the central role of myeloid lineage cells in coordinating the sepsis immune response.

Other immune cell types were also identified among the top matches. CD1C+ A Dendritic Cell Blood Human (distance: 3.948, cosine similarity: 0.724) was highly ranked, representing a dendritic cell subset involved in antigen presentation and T cell activation. B Cell Undefined Human (distance: 4.055, cosine similarity: 0.690) was also identified, indicating B cell involvement in the sepsis response, which may relate to antibody production and humoral immune responses. Eosinophil Blood Human (distance: 4.342, cosine similarity: 0.733) was present among the top matches, representing another granulocyte population involved in inflammatory responses.

The cell type analysis reveals a comprehensive picture of immune cell involvement in sepsis that aligns with established clinical understanding of the condition. The prominence of Th17 cells reflects the pro-inflammatory T cell response that contributes to tissue damage in severe sepsis, while the identification of exhausted T cell signatures suggests that prolonged immune activation may lead to T cell dysfunction, which is clinically relevant as T cell exhaustion has been associated with increased mortality in sepsis patients. The strong representation of myeloid cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, underscores the central role of innate immune cells in sepsis pathophysiology. Neutrophils are among the first responders to infection and are critical for bacterial clearance, but excessive neutrophil activation can contribute to organ damage through the release of reactive oxygen species and proteases. Monocytes and macrophages coordinate the immune response through cytokine production and antigen presentation, but dysregulated macrophage activation can contribute to the cytokine storm observed in severe sepsis. The identification of mast cells and basophils suggests that these granulocyte populations may also contribute to the inflammatory response, potentially through the release of histamine and other mediators. The presence of dendritic cell and B cell signatures indicates that both innate and adaptive immune responses are activated, reflecting the complex interplay between different arms of the immune system during sepsis. These findings support the clinical observation that sepsis involves activation of multiple immune cell populations across both innate and adaptive immunity, suggesting that therapeutic strategies targeting specific cell types, such as modulating T cell function or controlling myeloid cell activation, may need to be carefully balanced to preserve beneficial immune functions while controlling harmful inflammation.
