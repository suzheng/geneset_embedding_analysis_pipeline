# Results

## Immune Cell Types Involved in Sepsis

To identify which immune cell types are involved in the sepsis response, the sepsis-associated gene signature was compared against the CellMarker 2024 library, which contains 1,692 curated cell type marker signatures representing distinct immune and non-immune cell populations across human and mouse tissues. Cell type matching was performed using variance-weighted distance calculations across selected embedding dimensions, where smaller distance values indicate greater similarity between the sepsis signature and cell type marker signatures. The analysis revealed strong associations with multiple immune cell populations that play central roles in the inflammatory response during sepsis.

The highest-ranked cell type match was B Cell Undefined Human (distance: 3.250, cosine similarity: 0.809), indicating strong B cell involvement in the sepsis response. B cells contribute to humoral immunity through antibody production and antigen presentation, and their activation during sepsis may reflect the adaptive immune response to infection. The second-ranked match was T Helper 17 (Th17) Cell Undefined Human (distance: 3.278, cosine similarity: 0.865), a pro-inflammatory T cell subset that produces interleukin-17 and plays a critical role in host defense against extracellular pathogens. Th17 cells are known to be involved in severe inflammatory responses and have been implicated in sepsis pathophysiology. T Cell Undefined Mouse (distance: 3.775, cosine similarity: 0.795) was also among the top matches, indicating broad T cell involvement in the sepsis response.

Myeloid cell populations were prominently represented among the top matches. Monocyte signatures were highly ranked, with Monocyte Bone Marrow Mouse (distance: 3.491, cosine similarity: 0.792) and Monocyte Peripheral Blood Human (distance: 3.744, cosine similarity: 0.794) showing strong matches. M1 Macrophage Lung Human (distance: 3.515, cosine similarity: 0.858) was identified, reflecting the importance of classically activated macrophages in the pro-inflammatory immune response during sepsis. The broad Myeloid Cell Bone Marrow Human signature (distance: 3.906, cosine similarity: 0.781) and Myeloid Cell Bone Marrow Mouse signature (distance: 4.157, cosine similarity: 0.774) were identified, reflecting the central role of myeloid lineage cells in coordinating the sepsis immune response. Mast Cell Bone Marrow Mouse (distance: 3.463, cosine similarity: 0.811) and Basophil Undefined Human (distance: 3.749, cosine similarity: 0.804) were also highly ranked, representing granulocyte populations involved in allergic and inflammatory responses. Immune Cell Lung Human (distance: 3.923, cosine similarity: 0.785) was present among the top matches, indicating tissue-specific immune cell involvement.

The cell type analysis reveals a comprehensive picture of immune cell involvement in sepsis that aligns with established clinical understanding of the condition. The top-ranked B cell signature indicates that humoral immunity is activated during sepsis, which may relate to antibody production and B cell-mediated antigen presentation. The prominence of Th17 cells reflects the pro-inflammatory T cell response that contributes to tissue damage in severe sepsis. The strong representation of myeloid cells, including monocytes and M1 macrophages, underscores the central role of innate immune cells in sepsis pathophysiology. Monocytes and macrophages coordinate the immune response through cytokine production and antigen presentation, but dysregulated macrophage activation can contribute to the cytokine storm observed in severe sepsis. The identification of mast cells and basophils suggests that these granulocyte populations may also contribute to the inflammatory response, potentially through the release of histamine and other mediators. The presence of both B cell and T cell signatures indicates that both innate and adaptive immune responses are activated, reflecting the complex interplay between different arms of the immune system during sepsis. These findings support the clinical observation that sepsis involves activation of multiple immune cell populations across both innate and adaptive immunity, suggesting that therapeutic strategies targeting specific cell types may need to be carefully balanced to preserve beneficial immune functions while controlling harmful inflammation.
