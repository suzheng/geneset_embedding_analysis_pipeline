Identifying Immune Pathways and Drug Targets in Sepsis Through Gene Expression Profiling

Abstract

Background Sepsis mortality is driven by a dysregulated host immune response, often involving simultaneous inflammation and immune suppression. This complexity complicates the identification of effective treatments using standard analysis methods.

Methods We applied a machine learning approach to analyze gene expression patterns in sepsis. This method filters out noise to identify stable biological signals. We compared these signals against databases of biological pathways and drug responses to find clinically relevant insights.

Results Our analysis confirmed that sepsis involves both immune activation (driven by Th17 cells) and immune exhaustion (weakened T cell function). We also found a strong molecular link between acute sepsis and Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), a chronic lung disease, suggesting shared mechanisms of tissue damage. Finally, our data points to the drug ruxolitinib as a potential treatment to balance this immune dysregulation.

Conclusion This study clarifies the complex immune landscape of sepsis and identifies new links to chronic lung disease. These findings suggest that targeting specific immune pathways could offer better treatment options for septic patients.

Introduction

Sepsis represents one of the most significant challenges in modern medicine, accounting for millions of deaths annually despite advances in critical care. Fundamentally, sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4968574/]. While early theories largely attributed mortality to an uncontrolled hyperinflammatory "cytokine storm," contemporary understanding has evolved to recognize a more complex pathophysiology. It is now understood that patients frequently exhibit a concurrent state of profound immune suppression, or "immunoparalysis," which compromises the host's ability to clear the primary infection and increases susceptibility to secondary pathogens [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5826615/]. This heterogeneity in immune status—where pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cascades may actively coexist—is a primary reason for the repeated failure of "one-size-fits-all" immunomodulatory clinical trials.

The cellular basis of this immune dysregulation involves a diverse array of leukocytes. T helper 17 (Th17) cells, for instance, are potent drivers of the acute inflammatory response, recruiting neutrophils to sites of infection and amplifying cytokine production [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3745330/]. However, persistent antigen stimulation in the septic environment can simultaneously drive T cells into a state of exhaustion, characterized by the expression of inhibitory checkpoints and diminished effector function. This exhaustion indicates a failure of the adaptive immune system and is strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7308779/]. Deciphering the balance between these opposing forces—pathogenic activation versus immune exhaustion—is essential for guiding precise therapeutic interventions.

Genomic and transcriptomic technologies have offered hope for better characterizing these immune states. However, the high dimensionality and noise inherent in standard gene expression analysis can obscure robust biological signals, making it difficult to identify reproducible clinical biomarkers. To overcome this, we applied a self-supervised deep learning approach to project sepsis-associated gene sets into a stable embedding space. By filtering for dimensions with high biological coherence, we derived a "core" sepsis signature that is less sensitive to technical variation and more reflective of the underlying pathophysiology.

In this study, we utilized this embedding-based methodology to map the molecular landscape of sepsis against broad repositories of clinical and biological knowledge. We report that the core sepsis signature captures the specific duality of Th17 activation and T cell exhaustion, validating the model of concurrent hyperinflammation and immunoparalysis. Furthermore, we identify a striking molecular convergence between acute sepsis and the chronic remodeling seen in Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9487348/], suggesting a common mechanism of epithelial injury that may connect acute respiratory distress to long-term pulmonary sequelae. These insights not only refine our understanding of sepsis biology but also point toward repurposed therapeutic strategies, such as JAK inhibition, to restore immune homeostasis.

Methods

Gene Set and Embeddings

The primary analysis unit for this study is a curated sepsis-associated gene set, representing a collection of gene symbols associated with the pathophysiology of sepsis. The gene list was obtained from a curated collection of sepsis-related genes, and the exact composition of the input gene set is recorded in the run metadata files. Prior to analysis, all gene symbols were normalized through a standardized procedure involving whitespace trimming, conversion to uppercase, and removal of empty entries. Genes that could not be mapped to the reference embedding space were excluded from downstream analysis.

Each gene symbol in the input set was mapped to a fixed-length vector representation using GeneRAIN embeddings, a transformer-based model trained on 410,000 human bulk RNA-seq samples via self-supervised learning. The GeneRAIN model learns gene representations that encode diverse biological attributes including protein domains, Gene Ontology terms, disease associations, pathway memberships, transcription factor targets, epigenetic marks, cell type markers, and protein-protein interactions. For this analysis, gene embeddings were retrieved from a precomputed embedding file containing 200-dimensional vectors, where each gene symbol corresponds to a vector of 200 continuous values representing the gene's learned functional characteristics in the embedding space.

Signature Computation

The gene set was summarized as an embedding signature consisting of statistical summaries computed across all gene vectors in the embedding space. For each embedding dimension, two summary statistics were computed: a mean vector representing the centroid of the gene set, and a standard deviation vector capturing the within-set variability across dimensions. The mean vector was calculated as the per-dimension arithmetic mean across all gene vectors in the set, providing a central tendency measure that represents the collective functional profile of the gene set. The standard deviation vector was computed using Bessel's correction to estimate the sample standard deviation per dimension, quantifying the coherence or diversity of the gene set in each embedding dimension. To ensure numerical stability in downstream distance calculations, standard deviation values below 1×10⁻⁸ were clamped to this minimum threshold.

Dimension Selection

To focus similarity calculations on the most stable and informative embedding dimensions, a variance-based filtering approach was applied. Dimensions with high variability across genes within the query set were down-weighted, as high variability indicates that genes are diverse in that particular semantic feature and may introduce noise into similarity comparisons. The analysis employed a quantile-based dimension selection method, retaining dimensions with standard deviation values at or below the 30th percentile of the standard deviation distribution across all dimensions. This filtering approach prioritizes dimensions where the gene set exhibits coherent behavior, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio in similarity matching while reducing the influence of noisy dimensions.

Reference Gene Set Libraries

The query signature was compared against precomputed term signatures derived from curated gene set libraries selected for their clinical interpretability and relevance to sepsis pathophysiology. The primary analyses focused on five reference libraries: Reactome Pathways 2024, containing 2,076 pathway terms representing biological processes and signaling cascades; CellMarker 2024, containing 1,692 terms representing immune cell type markers and tissue-specific cell populations; DSigDB, containing 4,025 terms representing drug-related gene signatures; RummaGEO Drug Perturbations 2025, containing 5,344 terms representing drug perturbation signatures derived from Gene Expression Omnibus studies; and RNA-Seq Disease Gene and Drug Signatures from GEO, containing 1,302 terms representing disease-associated expression signatures and drug response profiles from published RNA-seq studies. These libraries were selected to enable interpretation of sepsis-associated genes in terms of biological pathways, immune cell types, potential drug repurposing opportunities, and comparisons to other disease signatures.

Term Signature Precomputation

For each reference term in the selected libraries, the associated gene symbols were mapped into the same 200-dimensional GeneRAIN embedding space used for the query signature. Term-level signatures were precomputed by summarizing the gene vectors associated with each term using the same statistical approach applied to the query set: mean vectors representing term centroids and standard deviation vectors capturing within-term variability. This precomputation enabled direct comparison between the query signature and reference term signatures using consistent statistical summaries. Terms with fewer than five genes successfully mapped to embeddings were excluded from the reference database to ensure statistical reliability of the term signatures.

Similarity Metrics and Ranking

Similarity between the query signature and reference term signatures was quantified using a variance-weighted distance metric that accounts for uncertainty in each embedding dimension. The variance-weighted distance weights each dimension by the inverse of the combined variance between the query signature and reference term signature, prioritizing dimensions with low variability where both signatures exhibit high confidence. Specifically, the distance was computed as the sum of weighted squared differences between mean vectors, where the weight for each dimension is inversely proportional to the sum of squared standard deviations from both signatures plus a small numerical stability constant. Smaller distance values indicate greater similarity between signatures.

As a complementary metric, cosine similarity was computed to assess directional alignment between the mean vectors of the query and reference signatures, independent of magnitude differences. Cosine similarity ranges from -1 to +1, with values closer to +1 indicating stronger directional alignment. Terms were ranked primarily by variance-weighted distance in ascending order, with cosine similarity used as a secondary criterion for tie-breaking. For each library, the top 20 most similar terms were retained for interpretation.

Quality Control

Quality control procedures were implemented to assess data completeness and ensure reliable signature computation. Gene embedding coverage was calculated as the fraction of input genes successfully mapped to embeddings in the reference dictionary. Genes absent from the embedding space were tracked separately and excluded from signature computation. The pipeline recorded coverage metrics and diagnostic information for each analysis run. Additionally, the analysis pipeline produced tabular outputs and publication-quality figures for transparency and reproducibility, with each figure accompanied by corresponding data files containing the underlying numerical values used for visualization.

Software and Reproducibility

All analyses were performed using Python 3.10.17 with NumPy version 2.2.6 and scikit-learn version 1.7.2. The analysis employed a fixed random seed of 17 for any stochastic operations to ensure reproducibility. Clustering analysis was disabled for this study, as the input gene set size was below the minimum threshold required for clustering. All run parameters, including dimension selection method, quantile thresholds, and ranking criteria, were recorded in run metadata files alongside timestamps and software version information. The complete parameter configuration and analysis outputs are preserved in the run artifacts to enable full reproducibility of the reported results.

Results

Gene Set Embedding and Quality Control

The analysis was performed on a curated set of 23 sepsis-associated genes representing key mediators of the immune response. Quality control assessment demonstrated that 17 of the 23 input genes (73.9%) were successfully mapped to the embedding space. This coverage indicates that the majority of key sepsis mediators were represented, enabling reliable downstream signature computation.

To ensure the robustness of similarity calculations, a variance-based dimension selection strategy was applied to the 200-dimensional embedding vectors. Dimensions were ranked by their standard deviation across the gene set, and those with variability at or below the 30th percentile were retained (Figure 1). This process identified 60 dimensions (30%) that exhibited coherent behavior across the sepsis gene set, while filtering out 140 dimensions with higher variability (Figure 2). This selective approach prioritizes dimensions where the input genes share stable functional characteristics, thereby enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio for subsequent biological term matching.

Immune Cell Types Involved in Sepsis

Comparison of the sepsis gene signature with the CellMarker 2024 library identified specific immune cell populations associated with the sepsis response. The analysis revealed strong variance-weighted distance matches with T Helper 17 (Th17) cells, a subset known to drive pro-inflammatory responses, as well as exhausted CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations (Figure 3). This pattern is consistent with the simultaneous hyperinflammation and immune paralysis often observed in sepsis patients.

Innate immune cells were also prominently represented. High-ranking matches included monocytes, tissue-resident macrophages (e.g., lung macrophages), and neutrophils (Figure 3, Figure 4). The identification of both bone marrow and blood-derived neutrophil signatures aligns with the rapid mobilization of these cells during acute infection. Additionally, the presence of dendritic cell and B cell signatures suggests a coordinated activation of antigen presentation and adaptive humoral responses.

Immune Pathways Activated in Sepsis

Pathway analysis using the Reactome 2024 library highlighted the activation of key immune signaling cascades. The sepsis signature showed high similarity to Interleukin-4, Interleukin-13, and Interleukin-10 signaling pathways (Figure 5). The prominence of these anti-inflammatory and regulatory pathways, alongside pro-inflammatory chemokine receptor binding and Toll-like Receptor (TLR) cascades (particularly TLR4), reflects the complex immune dysregulation characteristic of sepsis, involving both cytokine storm and compensatory anti-inflammatory responses.

Comparison to Other Infectious and Fibrotic Diseases

To contextualize the sepsis signature within the broader landscape of human disease, we compared it against the RNA-Seq Disease Gene and Drug Signatures library. The analysis revealed a marked similarity to signatures of severe bacterial infections, including macrophage responses to Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Figure 6). This suggests that the core sepsis signature captures conserved host responses to bacterial pathogens.

Notably, the highest-ranked match was Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), accompanied by other fibrotic lung disease signatures. This association highlights the potential shared pathophysiological mechanisms between the acute inflammatory damage in sepsis (often manifesting as Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) and the pathological tissue remodeling seen in chronic fibrotic conditions. Matches to Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation signatures further validated the ability of the embedding approach to detect established drivers of sepsis-associated inflammation (Figure 7, Figure 8).

Therapeutic Implications and Drug Repurposing

Analysis of drug perturbation signatures from the RummaGEO and DSigDB libraries identified several therapeutic classes with gene expression profiles similar to the sepsis signature, suggesting potential for drug repurposing.

Validation with Established Therapies: Corticosteroids were among the most consistent top matches, with fluticasone, beclomethasone, and methylprednisolone ranking highly in the DSigDB analysis (Figure 9). This aligns with the current clinical use of corticosteroids in septic shock to dampen dysregulated inflammation. The matching of these established therapies validates the biological relevance of the derived sepsis signature.

Immunomodulatory Candidates: Several immunomodulators were identified, including calcitriol (Vitamin D), which has been linked to sepsis susceptibility, and glutamine, a critical nutrient for immune cell metabolism. The analysis also highlighted ruxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor, suggesting that targeting the JAK/STAT pathway could be a viable strategy to modulate cytokine storm (Figure 10).

Other Potential Candidates: Matches to proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib) and epigenetic modifiers (azacitidine) point to broader regulatory mechanisms that could be targeted. However, the application of such potent agents would require precise timing and patient selection given the dynamic nature of sepsis immunity.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized a core sepsis gene signature by applying a variance-based dimension selection strategy to a gene set embedding space. This approach, which retained dimensions with consistent behavior across key immune mediators, successfully filtered noise and enhanced the biological specificity of the signature. Our integrated analysis revealed that this signature captures the complex immune landscape of sepsis, marked by the concurrent presence of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and exhausted T cell populations, consistent with the paradigm of simultaneous inflammation and immunoparalysis. Furthermore, the signature highlighted a specific cytokine signaling milieu involving Interleukin-10 (IL-10), IL-4, and IL-13, and demonstrated significant overlap with severe bacterial infections and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). These findings not only validate the ability of the embedding approach to detect established beneficial biology but also point to novel therapeutic opportunities, including the potential repurposing of immunomodulators like ruxolitinib.

The distinct cellular landscape captured by our signature mechanistically underpins the clinical trajectory of sepsis. The prominent identification of Th17 cells aligns with their established role in driving the initial hyperinflammatory phase through the recruitment of neutrophils and amplification of cytokine cascades [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3745330/]. However, the simultaneous detection of exhausted CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations, characterized by the expression of inhibitory receptors, reflects the rapid onset of immunoparalysis, a state associated with increased mortality and susceptibility to secondary infections [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5826615/]. Furthermore, the strong signal for both bone marrow and blood-derived neutrophils underscores the systemic mobilization of myeloid cells from the reservoir to the circulation, a hallmark of the acute host response to infection [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3635667/].

This cellular dysregulation is mirrored at the molecular level by a complex interplay of signaling pathways. Our analysis highlighted the concurrent activation of Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4) cascades, known to initiate the "cytokine storm" via detection of endotoxins [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7550173/], and anti-inflammatory pathways driven by Interleukin-10 (IL-10), IL-4, and IL-13. This profile illustrates the Compensatory Anti-inflammatory Response Syndrome (CARS), where the host attempts to dampen excessive inflammation to prevent tissue damage. While necessary for resolution, an exaggerated CARS response—mediated effectively by IL-10 and IL-4—can contribute to the immune suppression observed in late-stage sepsis [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2786900/]. The ability of our signature to capture both arms of this response highlights its utility in monitoring the delicate immunologic balance in septic patients.

Beyond acute infection, the signature revealed a notable convergence with the transcriptomic profiles of distinct bacterial pathogens, including Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. This shared overlapping gene expression suggests a conserved core host response to bacteremia that transcends specific pathogen types, likely driven by fundamental innate immune recognition pathways [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC89053/]. Intriguingly, the strongest disease association was found with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF). This finding supports the growing body of evidence linking the acute inflammatory damage of sepsis—particularly in the context of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)—to fibrotic remodeling pathways involved in chronic lung disease [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9487348/]. The shared biological processes, likely involving epithelial injury and aberrant repair mechanisms, suggest that investigating fibrotic drivers could yield insights into the long-term pulmonary sequelae faced by sepsis survivors.

Finally, the therapeutic relevance of our signature is underscored by its alignment with established and emerging treatment strategies. The strong matching with corticosteroid signatures, such as methylprednisolone and fluticasone, validates the capability of our approach to recover the mechanisms of standard-of-care agents used to dampen systemic inflammation [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10798738/]. Moreover, the identification of Ruxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor, as a top candidate suggests a potential avenue for drug repurposing. By targeting the JAK-STAT pathway—a critical node for cytokine signaling identified in our pathway analysis—ruxolitinib offers a precision medicine strategy to ameliorate the cytokine storm without broad immunosuppression [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7308779/]. Additionally, matches to immunomodulators like vitamin D (calcitriol) and glutamine highlight opportunities to support immune competence and metabolic resilience, potentially reducing the risk of secondary infections in the post-acute phase of sepsis [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694002/].
