# Functional Mapping of Sepsis Immune Dysregulation and Therapeutic Targets Using Large-Scale Gene Representation Learning

## Abstract

**Background** Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of dysregulated host response to infection that kills millions annually. It is marked by the coexistence of hyperinflammation and immune suppression—a paradox that has hindered effective immunomodulatory therapies. Genomic and transcriptomic studies are widely used to explore disease mechanisms, and gene set enrichment analysis is a common interpretive tool. However, conventional approaches rely on direct gene overlap and may miss biologically related gene sets that do not share the same gene symbols.

**Methods** We applied a gene representation–based framework in which sepsis-associated genes were converted into numerical embeddings learned from gene expression data of 410K samples. In this shared mathematical space, genes with similar biological functions cluster together even without direct overlap. We then compared the resulting sepsis functional signature with five curated reference libraries covering pathways, immune cell types, drug perturbation signatures, and disease profiles.

**Results** The sepsis signature showed strongest similarity to Th17 inflammatory programs, T cell exhaustion states, and innate immune cell populations, capturing the coexistence of hyperinflammation and immunosuppression. Disease signature analysis revealed molecular convergence with fibrotic and infection-related tissue injury programs. Drug signature analysis highlighted cytokine signaling and immune regulatory pathways as potential therapeutic targets.

**Conclusion** Gene representation–based analysis reveals concurrent Th17-driven inflammation and T cell exhaustion in sepsis, identifies molecular convergence with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, and supports JAK inhibition as a biologically rational therapeutic strategy.

## Introduction

Sepsis remains a major challenge in critical care, accounting for more than 11 million deaths worldwide each year and remaining a leading cause of in-hospital mortality despite decades of research {Rudd, 2020, Global, regional, and}. It is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection {Singer, 2016, The Third International}. Importantly, sepsis is not simply severe infection; it reflects a maladaptive immune response that drives organ failure.

Historically, sepsis was viewed primarily as a hyperinflammatory condition. An uncontrolled cytokine storm, triggered by innate recognition of pathogens, was believed to be the main driver of tissue injury and death {Chousterman, 2017, Cytokine storm and}. This concept led to multiple clinical trials targeting pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-1 {Marshall, 2014, Why have clinical}. Most failed to improve survival, indicating that hyperinflammation alone does not explain sepsis pathophysiology {Vincent, 2002, Clinical Trials of}.

Current understanding is more nuanced. Sepsis involves both excessive inflammation and profound immune suppression, often referred to as immunoparalysis {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}. These states can coexist within the same patient. Th17 cells amplify inflammation and recruit neutrophils, contributing to tissue injury in severe infection {Liu, 2021, Th17, rather than}. At the same time, persistent antigen exposure drives T cell exhaustion, in which CD4+ and CD8+ T cells progressively lose effector function and upregulate inhibitory receptors {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}. T cell exhaustion is strongly associated with secondary infections, prolonged ICU stay, and increased mortality {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}.

This dual immune state has major therapeutic implications. Broad anti-inflammatory therapy may worsen immunosuppression, while immune stimulation may aggravate organ damage in hyperinflamed patients. Failure to account for this heterogeneity is widely considered a key reason why promising immunomodulatory therapies have not translated into clinical success {Marshall, 2014, Why have clinical}. A clearer molecular understanding of these coexisting immune programs is therefore essential for precision treatment strategies {Peters van Ton, 2018, Precision Immunotherapy for}.

Large-scale genomic profiling has improved our ability to define immune states in sepsis {Scicluna, 2025, A consensus blood} and identify genes associated with susceptibility and outcome {Davenport, 2016, Genomic landscape of}. However, interpreting disease-associated gene lists remains difficult {Timmons, 2015, Multiple sources of}. Many gene sets are small, and individual genes often have multiple functions. Conventional enrichment methods depend on direct gene symbol overlap {Subramanian, 2005, Gene set enrichment}, which can miss functionally related gene sets that participate in the same pathway but do not share identical members across studies. Emerging large-scale models trained on population-level expression data provide a more comprehensive view of gene function across biological contexts {Theodoris, 2023, Transfer learning enables}.

To address these limitations, we applied an embedding-based framework trained on 410,000 human RNA-seq samples. In this model, each gene is represented as a point in a high-dimensional space learned from large-scale transcriptomic data. Genes with similar biological roles cluster together, even without direct gene overlap. By summarizing a gene set as a functional signature in this space, we can compare it systematically against thousands of reference gene sets representing pathways, cell types, diseases, and drug responses. Using this approach, we constructed a functional signature of sepsis-associated genes and mapped it across five curated biological reference libraries to define its immune context and therapeutic implications.
