# Functional Mapping of Sepsis Immune Dysregulation and Therapeutic Targets Using Large-Scale Gene Representation Learning

## Abstract

**Background** Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of dysregulated host response to infection that kills millions annually, characterized by the simultaneous occurrence of hyperinflammation and immune suppression—a duality that has frustrated efforts to develop effective immunomodulatory therapies. Genomic and transcriptomic studies have become central tools for uncovering disease mechanisms and identifying candidate therapeutics, and gene set enrichment analysis is among the most widely used methods for interpreting such data. However, conventional approaches rely on direct gene symbol overlap and therefore miss functional relationships between gene sets that share biological roles but not the same genes.

**Methods** We applied a gene representation-based computational framework in which sepsis-associated genes were translated into numerical representations learned from gene expression of 410K samples, enabling systematic discovery of robust functional relationships beyond direct gene overla. They position genes with similar biological functions close together in a shared mathematical space, and compared the resulting functional signature against five curated reference libraries covering biological pathways, immune cell types, drug signatures, and disease profiles.

**Results** The sepsis signature most closely matched Th17 inflammatory programs, T cell exhaustion states, and innate immune populations, capturing the coexistence of hyperinflammation and immunosuppression. Disease signature analysis demonstrated molecular convergence with fibrotic and infection-related tissue injury programs. Drug signature analysis highlighted cytokine signaling and immune regulatory pathways as potential therapeutic targets.

**Conclusion** Gene representation-based analysis of sepsis-associated genes reveals concurrent Th17-driven inflammation and T cell exhaustion, a molecular convergence with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, and nominates JAK inhibition as a rationale-supported therapeutic strategy.

## Introduction

Sepsis remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern critical care medicine, accounting for more than 11 million deaths annually worldwide and representing a leading cause of in-hospital mortality despite decades of intensive research {Rudd, 2020, Global, regional, and}. Defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection {Singer, 2016, The Third International}, sepsis is distinguished from uncomplicated infection not merely by the severity of the pathogen burden but by the maladaptive nature of the host immune response itself.

Early conceptual frameworks cast sepsis primarily as a hyperinflammatory disorder, in which an uncontrolled cytokine storm—driven by innate immune pattern recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns—was thought to be the principal cause of organ injury and death {Chousterman, 2017, Cytokine storm and}. This model motivated numerous clinical trials targeting individual pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor, interleukin-1, and high-mobility group box 1 protein {Marshall, 2014, Why have clinical}. With very few exceptions, these trials failed to improve mortality, suggesting that hyperinflammation alone did not capture the full complexity of sepsis pathophysiology {Vincent, 2002, Clinical Trials of}.

Contemporary understanding has substantially revised this view. It is now recognized that sepsis is characterized not by unidirectional hyperactivation but by a simultaneous and paradoxical combination of excessive inflammation and profound immune suppression, often termed immunoparalysis {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}. These opposing immune states may coexist within the same patient and even within the same tissue compartment, reflecting the extraordinary heterogeneity of the septic response. Pro-inflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells drive neutrophil recruitment and amplify cytokine production at sites of infection, contributing to collateral tissue damage in severe sepsis {Liu, 2021, Th17, rather than}. At the same time, persistent antigen stimulation in the septic environment can exhaust adaptive immune cells, particularly CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which progressively lose effector function and upregulate inhibitory checkpoint receptors {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}. T cell exhaustion is now recognized as a hallmark of sepsis-induced immunoparalysis and has been independently associated with secondary infections, prolonged intensive care unit stay, and increased mortality {Hotchkiss, 2013, Sepsis-induced immunosuppression: from}.

This immunological duality—active inflammation occurring concurrently with immune dysfunction—has profound implications for therapy. Strategies that broadly suppress inflammation risk deepening immunoparalysis, whereas approaches aimed at restoring immune function may exacerbate organ injury in patients who remain hyperinflamed. The failure to account for this heterogeneity is widely considered a central reason for the poor translation of promising preclinical immunomodulatory strategies into effective clinical therapies {Marshall, 2014, Why have clinical}. Identifying the molecular underpinnings of each immune state, and understanding how they interact, is therefore a prerequisite for developing precision therapeutic strategies in sepsis {Peters van Ton, 2018, Precision Immunotherapy for}.

Genomic studies and large-scale molecular profiling have substantially advanced our ability to characterize immune states in septic patients {Scicluna, 2025, A consensus blood} and to identify genes associated with disease susceptibility and outcome {Davenport, 2016, Genomic landscape of}. However, interpreting disease-associated gene sets remains challenging {Timmons, 2015, Multiple sources of} because individual genes often have multiple biological roles, and disease-associated gene lists are frequently small in number, limiting the ability of conventional methods to capture their shared functional context. Standard gene set enrichment methods, which assess whether a gene set is statistically overrepresented in a ranked list of differentially expressed genes or whether two gene sets share more members than expected by chance, depend critically on direct gene symbol overlap {Subramanian, 2005, Gene set enrichment}. These approaches may fail to capture functional similarity between gene sets that share biological roles—such as participation in the same signaling pathway or regulation of the same cellular process—but differ in their specific gene composition across studies or experimental contexts {Theodoris, 2023, Transfer learning enables}. Contemporary large-scale computational models now allow gene functions to be inferred from population-scale expression data, providing a more comprehensive and less bias-prone view of disease biology than traditional single-cohort analyses {Theodoris, 2023, Transfer learning enables}.

To overcome this limitation, we employed an embedding-based computational framework leveraging deep learning models trained on 410,000 human RNA-seq samples, enabling unbiased characterization of gene functional relationships across diverse biological contexts. In the model, each gene is represented as a point in a high-dimensional numerical space learned from large-scale human transcriptomic data. In this representation, genes with similar biological functions, co-expression patterns, and regulatory relationships are positioned close to one another in the embedding space, regardless of whether they share identical gene symbols. By summarizing a gene set as a statistical signature in this space—capturing both the collective functional profile and the internal coherence of the set—it becomes possible to compare a query gene set against thousands of reference gene sets representing pathways, cell types, diseases, and drug responses, even in the absence of direct gene overlap. In this study, we applied this framework to a curated set of sepsis-associated genes to construct a functional sepsis signature and map it systematically against five curated biological reference libraries. 
## Results

To investigate the biology of sepsis, we focused on genes with established genetic associations with the disease. Specifically, we used a curated set of 23 genes derived from a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of host genetic variants associated with sepsis susceptibility {Lu, 2019, Host genetic variants}. That study synthesized 349 publications evaluating 405 variants across 172 genes, identifying 29 variants within 23 genes with significant associations with sepsis risk—spanning pattern recognition receptors, cytokines, and other immune mediators central to pathogen sensing and host defense.

Although the individual functions of these genes are established, genes often carry multiple biological roles, and some of those roles may not be immediately apparent. To obtain a broader picture of the disease, we therefore examined these 23 genes collectively—asking what they share and how they relate to one another as a group. The most widely used approach for this purpose is gene set enrichment analysis, which asks whether the disease-associated gene set significantly overlaps with other gene sets of known biological function {Subramanian, 2005, Gene set enrichment}. However, when working with a relatively small number of genes, as in the present study, direct overlap-based methods are particularly limited: the probability that a small gene set shares exact gene symbols with any given reference set is low even when the underlying biology is closely related.

To overcome this limitation, we employed a numerical representation of genes in which genes with similar biological functions are assigned similar numerical values, enabling comparison by functional proximity rather than by gene symbol identity. These representations were derived from GeneRAIN, a deep learning model trained on 410,000 gene expression samples, which ensures that the learned representations are generalisable and robust {Su, 2025, GeneRAIN: multifaceted representation}. Rather than asking whether two gene sets share the same genes, this approach asks whether they occupy similar regions of a biologically meaningful space—a question that can be answered even when gene lists do not overlap at all.

After mapping the sepsis-associated genes into the embedding space, we next identified which dimensions most reliably reflected shared biological characteristics of this gene set. Although each gene is represented across 200 numerical dimensions, not all dimensions contribute equally to defining the common functional profile of sepsis. Some dimensions capture biological attributes that are consistently shared across the genes, whereas others reflect heterogeneity and may introduce noise into similarity comparisons.

To prioritize dimensions that represent coherent biological features, we applied a variance-based filtering strategy. Dimensions were ranked according to the variability of the sepsis genes within each dimension. Those with lower variability indicate that the genes behave similarly in that feature, suggesting a shared biological property. We retained dimensions at or below the 30th percentile of variability, corresponding to 60 of the 200 total dimensions. The remaining 140 dimensions, which showed greater heterogeneity across genes, were excluded from downstream similarity analyses in order to reduce noise and enhance interpretability {Bommert, 2021, Benchmark of filter}.

This high mapping rate indicates that the vast majority of genetically supported sepsis susceptibility genes were represented in the learned biological space, providing a robust foundation for subsequent analyses.

The resulting sepsis embedding signature—defined by the collective functional position of these genes across the 60 selected dimensions—serves as a quantitative representation of the core biological architecture of sepsis susceptibility. This signature was then used for systematic comparison against curated libraries of pathways, immune cell types, diseases, and drug perturbation profiles to identify biological processes and therapeutic hypotheses that share functional similarity with the sepsis genetic architecture {Milacic, 2023, The Reactome Pathway; Hu, 2022, CellMarker 2.0: an; Yoo, 2015, DSigDB: drug signatures; Marino, 2024, RummaGEO: Automatic mining; Wang, 2016, Extraction and analysis}.

By restricting comparisons to biologically coherent dimensions and ensuring high gene coverage, we aimed to maximize signal-to-noise ratio and ensure that downstream similarity findings reflect shared biological mechanisms rather than statistical artifact.

## Immune Cell Types Associated With the Sepsis Signature

To characterize the immune landscape associated with sepsis, we compared the sepsis genetic signature against the CellMarker reference library {Hu, 2022, CellMarker 2.0: an}, which contains curated marker profiles for 1,692 immune and non-immune cell populations. Cell type similarity was assessed using a variance-weighted distance metric in embedding space, where smaller distances indicate stronger functional similarity between the sepsis signature and reference cell type signatures.

The analysis revealed strong enrichment of both adaptive and innate immune cell populations, consistent with the complex immunological dysregulation observed in sepsis. Among adaptive immune cells, B cell signatures showed the strongest similarity to the sepsis signature (distance: 3.250; cosine similarity: 0.809), suggesting activation of humoral immune responses during sepsis. B cells contribute to host defense through antibody production and antigen presentation, and their activation may reflect ongoing pathogen recognition and immune adaptation.

T cell populations were also prominently represented. The sepsis signature showed strong similarity to T helper 17 (Th17) cells (distance: 3.278; cosine similarity: 0.865), a pro-inflammatory T cell subset that promotes neutrophil recruitment and amplifies cytokine signaling. 

Innate immune populations were also strongly represented. Monocyte signatures from bone marrow and peripheral blood compartments showed high similarity to the sepsis signature (Monocyte Bone Marrow Mouse: distance 3.491; Monocyte Peripheral Blood Human: distance 3.744). Macrophage signatures, particularly classically activated M1 macrophages from lung tissue, were also highly ranked (distance: 3.515; cosine similarity: 0.858), supporting the central role of pro-inflammatory myeloid activation in sepsis.

Additional innate immune components included mast cells and basophils, which are known to release histamine and inflammatory mediators during acute immune activation (Mast Cell Bone Marrow Mouse: distance 3.463; cosine similarity 0.811; Basophil Undefined Human: distance 3.749; cosine similarity 0.804). Tissue-level immune signatures were also observed, including lung immune cell signatures, reflecting organ-specific immune responses during systemic infection.

Overall, these findings demonstrate coordinated activation of both innate and adaptive immune compartments during sepsis, consistent with the dual inflammatory and immune-suppressive states observed clinically.

## Immune pathway signatures associated with sepsis

To characterize the biological functions represented by the sepsis susceptibility gene signature, we compared the sepsis-associated genes against the Reactome Pathways library {Milacic, 2023, The Reactome Pathway}. The sepsis signature showed strong enrichment for immune signaling pathways that are central to host inflammatory responses. The highest-ranked pathway was Interleukin-4 and Interleukin-13 Signaling (distance: 1.861, cosine similarity: 0.906), which is involved in anti-inflammatory regulation and alternative macrophage activation. This was followed by Interleukin-10 Signaling (distance: 2.334, cosine similarity: 0.901), a pathway known to suppress excessive cytokine production during systemic infection. The third-ranked pathway was Chemokine Receptors Bind Chemokines (distance: 3.252, cosine similarity: 0.838), reflecting mechanisms of leukocyte trafficking to infection sites.

Additional high-ranking pathways further illustrated the dual immune nature of sepsis. Cytokine signaling networks were broadly represented, including Signaling by Interleukins (distance: 3.651, cosine similarity: 0.807), Cytokine Signaling in Immune System (distance: 4.180, cosine similarity: 0.780), and Interleukin-6 Family Signaling (distance: 3.774, cosine similarity: 0.795), consistent with activation of acute inflammatory and acute phase responses.

Innate immune sensing pathways were also strongly represented. Toll-like receptor cascades were identified, including Toll-like receptor signaling (distance: 4.302, cosine similarity: 0.743) and Toll-like receptor 4 signaling (distance: 4.569, cosine similarity: 0.718), highlighting pathogen recognition mechanisms that initiate septic inflammation. Interferon-γ signaling (distance: 4.276, cosine similarity: 0.762) and inflammasome-related pathways (distance: 4.351, cosine similarity: 0.753) were also enriched, reflecting additional innate immune activation processes.

Collectively, these findings indicate that the sepsis genetic signature captures coordinated activation of pathogen recognition, cytokine signaling, and leukocyte recruitment pathways, consistent with the dysregulated systemic immune response observed clinically in sepsis.

### Disease Signature Similarity Analysis

To characterize the biological context of sepsis, we compared the functional signature derived from the sepsis-associated genes against 1,302 disease and perturbation signatures from RNA-Seq Disease Gene and Drug Signatures in GEO {Wang, 2016, Extraction and analysis}. Overall, sepsis demonstrated the strongest molecular similarity to severe infectious and fibrotic lung disease signatures, suggesting shared host response programs involving immune activation and tissue injury repair.

The highest-ranked match was idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) lung tissue (distance: 3.203; cosine similarity: 0.837). Clinically, this association is notable because both sepsis and IPF involve progressive epithelial damage and aberrant tissue remodeling. In sepsis, such processes are particularly relevant to patients who develop acute respiratory distress syndrome and long-term pulmonary fibrosis after prolonged mechanical ventilation.

The second-ranked match was Staphylococcus epidermidis macrophage response at 18 hours (distance: 3.276; cosine similarity: 0.817), reflecting strong similarity to innate immune activation triggered by gram-positive bacterial pathogens commonly associated with healthcare-associated infections. Similar patterns were observed for Yersinia pseudotuberculosis macrophage response signatures (distance: 3.362; cosine similarity: 0.815), representing host responses to gram-negative bacterial invasion.

Multiple bacterial infection response signatures were consistently enriched among top-ranking matches. Salmonella typhimurium macrophage responses at 18 hours and 48 hours were both highly ranked (distance range: 3.395–3.497; cosine similarity range: 0.803–0.807), reflecting conserved transcriptional programs associated with systemic bacterial dissemination and host defense. Likewise, Mycobacterium tuberculosis macrophage response signatures were repeatedly identified, including H37Rv and GC1237 strains across 18-hour and 48-hour time points (distance range: 3.489–3.785; cosine similarity range: 0.783–0.801). These results suggest that sepsis shares transcriptional characteristics with both acute and persistent intracellular bacterial infections that require sustained immune activation.

Additional infection-related signatures included heat-inactivated Mycobacterium tuberculosis responses and Staphylococcus epidermidis early response signatures, indicating that pathogen-derived molecular components alone can induce expression patterns similar to those observed in sepsis.

Beyond bacterial infection signatures, immune stimulation by pathogen-associated molecular patterns was also observed. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophage signatures were highly ranked (distance range: 3.908–3.966; cosine similarity: 0.775), consistent with the central role of LPS-triggered inflammatory cascades in sepsis pathogenesis. Bacillus Calmette-Guérin stimulation signatures were also present among high-ranking matches (distance range: 3.859–3.885), further supporting the involvement of innate immune activation pathways.

Finally, idiopathic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia lung tissue signatures were identified among top matches (distance: 4.055; cosine similarity: 0.765), further supporting molecular overlap between acute septic lung injury and chronic fibrotic lung remodeling.

### Drug Repurposing Analysis Identifies Immunomodulatory and Anti-inflammatory Therapeutic Candidates

To identify potential therapeutic opportunities, the sepsis-associated gene functional signature was compared against the DSigDB drug signature library {Yoo, 2015, DSigDB: drug signatures} containing 4,025 compound-related gene expression signatures. Drug matching was performed using embedding-based functional similarity metrics, where smaller distance values and higher cosine similarity indicate stronger biological similarity between drug-induced transcriptional patterns and the sepsis signature.

The top-ranked drug match was POVPC (distance: 1.761, cosine similarity: 0.912), an oxidized phosphatidylcholine lipid mediator with anti-inflammatory properties. This was followed by D-Monapterin (distance: 1.939, cosine similarity: 0.897), a compound involved in pteridine metabolism. Inhaled corticosteroids fluticasone (distance: 2.108, cosine similarity: 0.890) and beclomethasone (distance: 2.192, cosine similarity: 0.882) ranked third and fourth, respectively. Additional high-ranking matches included clodronate disodium (distance: 2.299, cosine similarity: 0.883), an osteoclast activity inhibitor with immunomodulatory effects, and sulfasalazine (distance: 2.380, cosine similarity: 0.875), a disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug used in inflammatory diseases.

Additional candidate compounds included parthenolide (distance: 2.432, cosine similarity: 0.863), DL-mevalonic acid (distance: 2.495, cosine similarity: 0.871), ferrous sulfate (distance: 2.529, cosine similarity: 0.876), and dinoprostone (distance: 2.548, cosine similarity: 0.871). Overall, the drug matching pattern was dominated by anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and metabolic pathway-related compounds, suggesting that multiple biological processes contribute to sepsis immune dysregulation.

## Functional Mapping of Sepsis Gene Signature to Drug Perturbation Signatures

To further identify potential therapeutic compounds associated with the sepsis molecular signature, we also compared the sepsis-associated gene signature against 5,344 drug perturbation signatures from the RummaGEO Drug Perturbations library {Marino, 2024, RummaGEO: Automatic mining}, which contains experimentally derived transcriptional responses to drug exposure. The analysis identified several clinically relevant compounds with strong transcriptional similarity to the sepsis signature, suggesting potential drug repurposing opportunities for immune modulation in sepsis.

The highest-ranked match was glutamine (distance: 3.002, cosine similarity: 0.769), an amino acid that plays a central role in immune cell metabolism and cytokine regulation. This finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating altered glutamine metabolism during critical illness and sepsis. Clinical trials of glutamine supplementation in critically ill patients have reported heterogeneous outcomes, reflecting the context-dependent nature of immune metabolic support in sepsis.

The second-ranked match was temozolomide (distance: 3.391, cosine similarity: 0.742), an alkylating chemotherapeutic agent primarily used in brain tumor treatment. Although not directly related to sepsis pathogenesis, temozolomide is known to induce immunosuppression through cytokine and lymphocyte regulatory effects, which may explain partial transcriptional similarity with the sepsis signature.

The third-ranked compound was bortezomib (distance: 3.448, cosine similarity: 0.739), a proteasome inhibitor that modulates nuclear factor-kappa B signaling and inflammatory cytokine production. Previous experimental sepsis models have shown context-dependent effects of proteasome inhibition on inflammatory injury, likely reflecting the dual inflammatory and immunosuppressive nature of sepsis.

Calcitriol, the active form of vitamin D, appeared twice among the top-ranked signatures, with similarity scores of distance 3.465 (cosine similarity 0.737) and 3.602 (cosine similarity 0.720) under different experimental conditions. Vitamin D signaling is known to influence innate and adaptive immune responses, and vitamin D deficiency has been associated with increased susceptibility to infection and poorer outcomes in critically ill patients.

Ruxolitinib, a Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor used in myeloproliferative disorders, ranked fifth (distance: 3.496, cosine similarity: 0.720). Ruxolitinib suppresses JAK/STAT-mediated cytokine signaling, including interleukin-6 and interferon-mediated pathways. Preclinical models of hyperinflammatory syndromes have demonstrated that JAK inhibition can reduce cytokine storm responses and attenuate organ injury when administered in appropriate therapeutic windows.

Additional high-ranking matches included curcumin (distance: 3.594, cosine similarity: 0.755), azacitidine (distance: 3.633, cosine similarity: 0.736), decitabine (distance: 3.658, cosine similarity: 0.732), and berberine (distance: 3.712, cosine similarity: 0.722). These compounds represent diverse pharmacological classes but share common biological themes of immune regulation, inflammatory suppression, or antimicrobial activity.

Overall, drug perturbation mapping revealed enrichment of compounds targeting metabolic regulation, epigenetic modification, and cytokine signaling pathways, supporting the hypothesis that sepsis pathophysiology reflects integrated dysregulation across multiple immune regulatory axes.

# Discussion

The central challenge in sepsis research has never been a shortage of candidate targets—it has been an incomplete understanding of how those targets relate to one another within the broader immune landscape of a patient who is simultaneously fighting an infection and destroying their own tissues {van der Poll, 2017, The immunopathology of}. Our findings, generated through a gene representation-based approach applied to 23 genetically validated sepsis susceptibility genes, offer a functionally integrated view of this immune dysregulation and point toward therapeutic strategies that respect its complexity.

## The Dual Immune Signature Reflects Clinical Reality

The most clinically meaningful observation from this analysis is that the sepsis genetic signature simultaneously encodes features of active inflammation and immune exhaustion—not as competing signals, but as co-existing biological realities {Hotchkiss, 2013, Immunosuppression in sepsis:}. The strong functional similarity to Th17 cell populations and M1 macrophages on one hand, and to exhausted T cell programs on the other, mirrors exactly what intensivists observe at the bedside: patients mounting an inflammatory response vigorous enough to damage their own organs while simultaneously failing to clear the infection that triggered it {van der Poll, 2017, The immunopathology of}.

This co-existence likely explains many large trial failures {Marshall, 2014, Why have clinical}. Studies targeting individual pro-inflammatory mediators enrolled patients without stratifying by immune state. If Th17-driven inflammation and T cell exhaustion co-exist in the same patient—encoded within the same genetic architecture, as our results suggest—then a therapy suppressing one without addressing the other would be expected to produce, at best, a neutral effect on mortality. The right therapeutic frame may therefore not be "suppress the cytokine storm" or "reverse the immunoparalysis" but rather "restore the regulatory balance between these systems."

The Reactome pathway analysis reinforces this picture. Top-ranked anti-inflammatory pathways, including IL-10 and IL-4/IL-13 signaling, likely reflect the immune system's own counter-regulatory attempts rather than a paradox. Their co-enrichment alongside TLR4 cascades, IL-6 family signaling, and chemokine receptor pathways paints a portrait of a system operating at high amplitude in both directions simultaneously {van der Poll, 2017, The immunopathology of}.

## The IPF Connection Illuminates Organ Injury Mechanisms

One of the more striking findings is the molecular convergence between the sepsis signature and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. This overlap becomes intuitive when considered from the perspective of tissue injury and repair: both conditions involve progressive epithelial damage, aberrant tissue remodeling, and failure to restore normal organ architecture after an inflammatory insult. In sepsis, this is most directly relevant to acute respiratory distress syndrome, which shares key pathophysiological features with early IPF—including alveolar epithelial injury and dysregulated fibroblast activation {Burnham, 2013, The fibroproliferative response}. The additional enrichment of nonspecific interstitial pneumonia signatures further reinforces the molecular affinity between septic lung injury and fibrotic remodeling. This raises the possibility that antifibrotic agents may warrant investigation in the recovery phase of sepsis-associated lung injury, when fibrotic remodeling becomes the dominant pathological process.

## JAK Inhibition as a Rationally Supported Therapeutic Strategy

The convergence of evidence from two independent drug signature libraries toward JAK-STAT pathway inhibition provides the most actionable clinical insight from this analysis. Ruxolitinib appeared as a high-ranking match in the RummaGEO drug perturbation analysis, and the Reactome enrichment of IL-6 and interferon signaling—both of which signal through the JAK-STAT axis {Hu, 2021, The JAK/STAT signaling}—provides the mechanistic rationale for this alignment. JAK inhibition would be expected to attenuate both the inflammatory and the T cell exhaustion-promoting effects of sustained cytokine exposure, addressing both immune states simultaneously rather than one in isolation {Biwott, 2025, Ruxolitinib Modulates P-Glycoprotein}.

This hypothesis is not without clinical precedent. JAK inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in cytokine storm syndromes and in hyperinflammatory presentations of COVID-19, where baricitinib and ruxolitinib reduced mortality and ventilator requirements in selected patients {Kalil, 2021, Baricitinib plus Remdesivir}. The biological distance between those syndromes and sepsis-associated hyperinflammation is smaller than their diagnostic categories suggest, and the functional similarity identified here adds computational weight to the case for evaluating JAK inhibition in carefully selected sepsis patients with documented hyperinflammatory phenotypes.

The enrichment of metabolic pathway-targeting compounds—glutamine, calcitriol, mevalonic acid—in the drug analysis reflects the growing recognition that immune dysfunction in sepsis is shaped not only by cytokine dysregulation but by profound metabolic reprogramming in activated and exhausted immune cells, an axis that deserves greater attention in therapeutic development {Koutroulis, 2019, Sepsis Immunometabolism: From; Xian, 2026, Metabolic reprogramming tailors}.

## Methodological Considerations and Limitations

A key strength of this analysis is that it achieves biologically coherent results from a relatively small input gene set precisely because embedding-based comparisons do not require gene symbol overlap to detect functional similarity. The variance-filtering step, which retained embedding dimensions reflecting shared biological properties across sepsis genes, further enhanced signal specificity, and the 91.3% gene coverage rate confirms that the functional signature is not driven by a small subset of over-represented genes.

Nonetheless, the input gene set reflects associations with sepsis susceptibility rather than severity or specific clinical phenotypes, and genes governing susceptibility may overlap only partially with those driving organ injury or mortality {Weng, 2025, KEY GENETIC DETERMINANTS}. Drug signature libraries also represent transcriptional responses measured under specific experimental conditions that may not fully recapitulate the in vivo septic environment. Findings should therefore be interpreted as mechanistically grounded hypotheses requiring prospective experimental and clinical validation.

## Conclusion

The sepsis genetic architecture encodes simultaneous features of Th17-driven inflammation and T cell exhaustion—a combination that explains the failure of unidirectional immunomodulatory strategies and that demands therapeutic approaches capable of restoring immune balance. The molecular convergence with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis highlights tissue remodeling as an underappreciated axis of sepsis pathophysiology. And the consistent nomination of JAK-STAT inhibition across independent analyses, grounded in the pathway-level enrichment of IL-6 and interferon signaling, offers a mechanistically coherent and clinically testable therapeutic hypothesis. For the clinician at the bedside, these results reaffirm that sepsis immune dysregulation is not a single failure but a systemic collapse of immune regulation across multiple axes—and that therapeutic success will likely require addressing that complexity in full.## Methods

### Gene Set and Embeddings

The primary analytical unit of this study was a curated set of 23 genes representing host genetic determinants of sepsis susceptibility {Lu, 2019, Host genetic variants}. By focusing on genes supported by aggregated human genetic evidence rather than individual transcriptomic experiments, we aimed to construct a biologically grounded representation of the host susceptibility architecture of sepsis. This strategy ensures that the subsequent embedding-based analysis reflects core genetic contributors to sepsis risk, rather than context-specific or transient expression changes.

To enable functional comparison of this gene set against diverse biological reference databases—including pathways, cell type markers, disease signatures, and drug response profiles—we employed gene embeddings as the core representational framework. Gene embeddings are dense numerical vectors learned from large-scale transcriptomic data such that genes with similar biological functions, co-expression patterns, and regulatory relationships occupy nearby positions in a shared mathematical space {Du, 2019, Gene2vec: distributed representation}. This approach offers a critical advantage over conventional overlap-based enrichment methods: it can identify functional similarity between gene sets even when those sets share few or no gene symbols in common, thereby capturing biological relationships that direct overlap metrics would miss.

Embedding vectors were retrieved from GeneRAIN, a transformer-based model trained via self-supervised learning on 410,000 human bulk RNA-seq samples {Su, 2025, GeneRAIN: multifaceted representation}. The model generates 200-dimensional vector representations for each gene, encoding a rich array of biological attributes including protein domain membership, Gene Ontology annotations, disease associations, pathway memberships, transcription factor target relationships, epigenetic marks, cell-type-specific expression patterns, and protein-protein interaction partners. Embeddings were retrieved from a precomputed dictionary containing 200-dimensional vectors for 31,769 human genes. Genes not present in this dictionary were excluded from downstream signature computation.

### Signature Computation

[too long, too technical: To enable comparison of the sepsis gene set against reference libraries, the set of individual gene embedding vectors was summarized as a compact statistical signature capturing both the collective functional profile and the internal coherence of the gene set across embedding dimensions.] For each of the 200 embedding dimensions, two summary statistics were computed: a mean vector and a standard deviation vector. The mean vector represents the per-dimension arithmetic mean across all embedded gene vectors in the set, providing a centroid that encodes the average functional characteristics of the gene set in the embedding space. The standard deviation vector was computed using Bessel's correction and quantifies the degree to which individual genes in the set agree or diverge in each semantic dimension: a low standard deviation in a given dimension indicates that all genes share a common attribute encoded by that dimension, whereas a high standard deviation indicates that genes are heterogeneous with respect to that attribute and that the dimension may contribute noise to similarity calculations. To ensure numerical stability in downstream distance computations, standard deviation values below 1×10⁻⁸ were clamped to this minimum threshold.

### Dimension Selection

Because the 200 embedding dimensions vary substantially in how coherently the sepsis gene set behaves across them, we applied a variance-based dimension selection step prior to similarity matching. The rationale for this step is straightforward: dimensions in which the sepsis genes show high internal variability reflect biological attributes for which the genes are heterogeneous, and including such dimensions in similarity calculations introduces noise that may obscure genuine functional relationships. Conversely, dimensions in which the genes show low variability reflect shared biological attributes—precisely those features most likely to drive meaningful similarity with reference gene sets.

Dimension selection was implemented by ranking all 200 dimensions by their within-set standard deviation values and retaining those at or below the 30th percentile of this distribution. This quantile-based filtering identified 60 dimensions exhibiting the most coherent behavior across the sepsis gene set. The remaining 140 dimensions, characterized by higher within-set variability, were excluded from downstream similarity matching. All subsequent comparisons between the sepsis signature and reference term signatures were conducted exclusively within these 60 selected dimensions.

### Reference Gene Set Libraries

To interpret the sepsis signature in terms of established biology, we compared it against term signatures derived from five curated reference gene set libraries selected for their breadth of coverage and clinical relevance to sepsis pathophysiology. Reactome Pathways 2024 contains 2,076 terms representing well-annotated biological processes and signaling cascades and was used to identify pathway-level associations {Milacic, 2023, The Reactome Pathway}. CellMarker 2024 contains 1,692 terms representing curated cell type marker gene sets across human and mouse tissues and was used to identify immune cell populations associated with the sepsis signature {Hu, 2022, CellMarker 2.0: an}. DSigDB contains 4,025 drug-related gene signatures and was used for initial drug repurposing queries {Yoo, 2015, DSigDB: drug signatures}. RummaGEO Drug Perturbations 2025 contains 5,344 drug perturbation signatures derived from publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus studies and was used for complementary drug repurposing analysis {Marino, 2024, RummaGEO: Automatic mining}. RNA-Seq Disease Gene and Drug Signatures from GEO contains 1,302 disease-associated expression signatures from published RNA-seq studies and was used to identify diseases sharing molecular characteristics with the sepsis signature {Wang, 2016, Extraction and analysis}.

### Term Signature Precomputation and Similarity Matching

To enable efficient and consistent similarity search across the thousands of terms in each reference library, signatures were precomputed for every reference term using the identical statistical framework applied to the query gene set. For each term, the associated gene symbols were mapped into the GeneRAIN embedding space, and mean and standard deviation vectors were computed across the resulting gene vectors. Terms with fewer than five genes successfully mapped to the embedding dictionary were excluded from the reference database to ensure that term signatures were based on sufficient information for reliable computation.

Similarity between the sepsis query signature and each reference term signature was assessed using a variance-weighted distance metric. This metric accounts for uncertainty in the standard deviation vectors across each embedding dimension. In this metric, each embedding dimension is weighted by the inverse of the combined variance from both the query and reference term signatures {Zarrella, 2011, Host-Adaptation of Francisella}: dimensions where both signatures exhibit low variability—and therefore high confidence—contribute more strongly to the distance calculation, while dimensions where either signature is uncertain are down-weighted. Formally, the distance was computed as the sum of weighted squared differences between mean vectors, where the weight for each dimension is inversely proportional to the sum of squared standard deviations from both signatures plus a small numerical stability constant. This approach ensures that similarity judgments are driven by dimensions of mutual confidence rather than dimensions dominated by noise.

As a complementary metric, cosine similarity between the mean vectors of the query and reference signatures was computed to assess directional alignment independent of magnitude differences, providing a useful secondary measure of functional concordance. Terms were ranked primarily by variance-weighted distance in ascending order, with cosine similarity used for tie-breaking. For each library, the top 20 most similar terms were retained for biological interpretation.

### Quality Control and Software

To ensure that downstream results reflected genuine biological signal rather than artifacts of data incompleteness, quality control metrics were recorded for each analysis run. Gene embedding coverage was calculated as the fraction of input genes successfully mapped to the GeneRAIN dictionary. The analysis recorded coverage metrics, dimension selection statistics, including dimension selection quantile thresholds, numerical stability constants, and ranking criteria, to enable full reproducibility.

All analyses were performed using Python 3.10.17 with NumPy version 2.2.6 {Harris, 2020, Array programming with} and scikit-learn version 1.7.2. A fixed random seed of 17 was used for all stochastic operations. Clustering analysis was not performed, as the input gene set size was below the minimum threshold required for this procedure. Analysis outputs, parameter configurations, and run metadata are preserved in full to enable reproduction of all reported results.
